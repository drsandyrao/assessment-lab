Round 3 · Client role

You are Evelyn Bennett

What you say first

“I'm managing. My wrist is a nuisance, but it'll heal. Joanne's at home, so I'm not going back to an empty house or anything. I really don't think I need a whole plan made for me.”

How to play the client

Begin with only the opening statement.

When your partner asks about an area of your life, open the relevant section below.

Answer using the information provided. You do not need to tell them everything in the section at once. If they follow up, you can share more.

You may elaborate naturally when it is consistent with the scenario.

If information is not provided, you can say:

  • “I'm not sure.”
  • “I don't really know.”
  • “I'd rather not get into that right now.”
  • “Can you tell me why you're asking?”

Never use your own personal experiences to fill gaps in the scenario.

Your scenario information

Open a section when the assessor asks about that part of your life. Follow the cues inside each section.

Everyday life

If asked:

You are 68, a retired elementary-school teacher, enthusiastic baker, gardener and crossword solver. You read constantly, watch terrible murder mysteries, volunteer with a literacy program and lunch monthly with former colleagues. You and Joanne have a dog named Archie.

Home and current functioning

If asked:

You and Joanne own a mortgage-free townhouse where you have lived for 14 years. You want to remain there. Stairs and tasks such as cooking, bathing and driving are temporarily harder while your fractured wrist heals.

If they follow up:

You expect to recover and dislike being treated as frail. You are worried about depending on Joanne while you heal.

Marriage and recent changes

If asked generally:

You and Joanne, 71, have been together for 22 years and married for 11. There is real love and a long shared history.

If they follow up about changes:

Over two years Joanne has become irritable and suspicious. She checks where you go, dislikes some friends and accuses you of hiding things or having an affair. You reduced social activities to avoid arguments. This is not how most of the relationship has been.

Current safety

If asked whether you feel safe:

You pause and say, “Mostly.” If followed up: “I don't think she's going to seriously hurt me. But I don't like how things have been lately.” You have felt afraid a couple of times.

If asked behaviourally:

Joanne has taken your phone and read messages, stood in doorways so you could not leave until an argument was “finished,” and called you selfish and a liar. Two months ago she grabbed your forearm hard enough to bruise, then apologized and cried.

Threats and weapons:

No firearms. Joanne has not threatened to kill you, herself or anyone else. You do not believe you are in immediate danger.

The fall

If asked directly:

Joanne did not push you. You were carrying laundry, missed a stair and fell. Joanne called the ambulance.

How questions affected you:

Repeated questions about being pushed made you feel clinicians had decided what happened before listening.

Joanne's health

If asked what seems different:

Over the past year Joanne repeats questions, misplaces items and blames others, sometimes becomes confused driving, struggles with online banking, grows suspicious and forgets conversations.

If they follow up:

There is no diagnosis. Joanne gets angry when you suggest seeing a physician. Possible cognitive change worries you and may provide context, but it does not make controlling or frightening behaviour acceptable.

Sexual consent and pressure

If asked only whether Joanne sexually assaulted you:

You say no.

If asked behaviourally about unwanted sex or pressure:

Several months ago you declined sex. Joanne became upset and accused you of wanting someone else. You eventually had sex so the argument would stop. You say, “I wouldn't say she forced me exactly. I just… it was easier.”

What the assessor needs:

They may ask enough to understand consent, coercion and current safety. You do not need to provide graphic details.

Earlier sexual violence

If asked appropriately about previous unwanted sexual experiences:

You may say, “Something happened when I was young.” At 24 you were sexually assaulted by a man you knew. Joanne knows. You do not see it as the explanation for the present.

If asked for details you do not want to provide:

“I don't think you need the whole story.”

Family

If asked:

Your daughter, Simone, is 41 and lives 30 minutes away. You are close and have two grandchildren. They call Joanne “Jo.”

If they follow up:

Simone knows Joanne has become “difficult” but not about grabbing, doorway blocking or sexual pressure. You worry she would immediately insist you leave. You do not want her to take over.

Money and resources

If asked:

You have a teacher's pension, retirement savings, stable housing and no immediate financial insecurity. There is no evidence Joanne is financially abusing you.

If they follow up:

Joanne historically managed banking. You now check accounts because of her memory changes. Financial security gives you options, but does not resolve the safety concerns.

Health and emotional wellbeing

If asked:

Besides the wrist, you have controlled hypertension and knee osteoarthritis. You are normally independent and have no cognitive diagnosis.

Emotional wellbeing:

You do not describe yourself as depressed and have no suicidal thoughts or self-harm history. You are anxious, sleep somewhat worse and carefully plan how to raise ordinary topics to avoid arguments.

Identity and experiences with services

If asked:

You are a white Canadian cisgender lesbian. You were previously married to a man, cared about him and had Simone. You came out later; you do not describe the earlier marriage as fake.

Institutional experiences:

Earlier in life you were cautious about disclosing you were lesbian. At the hospital, someone questioned Joanne instead of you and another called her your “friend” after learning you were married. You also dislike age-based patronizing.

Supports, strengths and what matters

If asked:

You have friends, Simone, former colleagues, community connections, stable housing, financial resources and strong system-navigation skills. You spent decades advocating for children and families.

What matters:

Autonomy, your marriage, family, home, friendships, community, Joanne's dignity and making your own decisions. You love Joanne; that does not erase the concerning behaviour.

What I want

If asked:

“I need things at home to change.” You do not currently say you want to leave Joanne.

If they follow up:

You want safety, medical assessment for Joanne, an end to monitoring and accusations, temporary help while your wrist heals, continued friendships, and professionals to take this seriously without deciding for you. You are willing to discuss staying elsewhere temporarily but have not decided.

After the conversation

When the instructor calls time, use these prompts for paired or verbal reflection. Nothing is collected here.

  • What did the assessor's questions invite you to talk about?
  • What parts of your life stayed invisible?
  • Did you understand why you were being asked these questions?
  • Was there something you were waiting for them to ask?
