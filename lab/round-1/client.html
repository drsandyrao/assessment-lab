Round 1 · Client role

You are Maya Chen

What you say first

“I've just been really overwhelmed lately. I'm usually pretty good at keeping everything together, but the last few months have been a lot. I'm tired all the time, I'm not sleeping properly, and I feel like I'm falling behind on everything.”

How to play the client

Begin with only the opening statement.

When your partner asks about an area of your life, open the relevant section below.

Answer using the information provided. You do not need to tell them everything in the section at once. If they follow up, you can share more.

You may elaborate naturally when it is consistent with the scenario.

If information is not provided, you can say:

  • “I'm not sure.”
  • “I don't really know.”
  • “I'd rather not get into that right now.”
  • “Can you tell me why you're asking?”

Never use your own personal experiences to fill gaps in the scenario.

Your scenario information

Open a section when the assessor asks about that part of your life. Follow the cues inside each section.

Everyday life

If asked generally:

You are 29, funny, organized and social. You enjoy cooking, live music, restaurants, podcasts and long walks with Dani. Your university friends have an active group chat. You give Dani running commentary on a complicated television baking competition.

What is ordinary about you:

You remember birthdays, make reservations and organize plans. You usually describe this simply as: “I just get things done.”

Home and relationship

If asked:

You have lived with your girlfriend, Dani, for two years and have been together for four. The relationship is affectionate and generally supportive. You feel safe at home.

If they follow up:

Dani sees how exhausted you are and encourages you to slow down. Sometimes you argue because it feels like one more person telling you what to do. Money has also created some tension.

Family and caregiving

If asked generally:

Your parents live about 40 minutes away. Your older brother lives in Alberta. You love your family.

If they follow up:

Your father has recent, manageable health problems but many appointments. You accompany him, track information and help your parents understand it. Your mother avoids highway driving and your brother cannot provide much practical help from Alberta. You never formally agreed to coordinate everything; it gradually became your job. You value caring for them and are also exhausted by it.

Work

If asked generally:

You work full time as a communications coordinator for a mid-sized nonprofit. You like the work, especially writing and campaigns, and you are good at it.

If they follow up:

After restructuring, an unreplaced colleague's duties were added to yours. You work through lunch and sometimes log back in after dinner. Your supervisor calls you “so dependable,” which feels like praise and a trap. You have taken three unusual sick days in six weeks and worry colleagues think you cannot cope.

Money and housing

If asked generally:

You and Dani can pay your bills and your housing is stable. You would initially say you are financially “fine.”

If they ask specifically about pressure:

Your rent increased, you have substantial student loans, and you sometimes pay expenses for your parents without discussing it much with Dani. You worry about money, but there is no food insecurity or imminent housing loss.

Physical health, pain and functioning

If asked about health:

You have endometriosis, with pelvic pain, fatigue and sometimes severe menstrual pain. Some months are worse than others. Poor sleep and stress are making pain harder to manage.

If asked whether you are disabled:

You would probably say, “No.” If asked instead about chronic conditions, pain or effects on daily activity, you explain much more. You were dismissed for years before diagnosis but now reasonably trust your physician.

Emotional wellbeing and sleep

If asked generally:

You feel overwhelmed and irritable and sometimes cry unexpectedly after getting home. You worry at night about work, your father, money and disappointing people.

If they follow up:

Your mind runs through tasks when you try to sleep. You do not describe yourself as depressed and still enjoy things when you have time. You have no current diagnosis or psychiatric medication. Brief counselling in university was somewhat helpful.

Thoughts of death and self-harm

If asked directly about suicide:

“No, I haven't been thinking about killing myself.” You have no plan, intent or attempt history.

If asked about wanting to disappear or not exist:

Two weeks ago, after a very difficult day, you thought, “I wish I could just stop existing for a while.” You wanted the demands to stop; you did not want to die or consider killing yourself.

If asked about self-harm:

You do not self-harm and have no history of intentional self-injury.

Alcohol, cannabis and other substances

If asked:

You drink socially, usually one or two drinks. You sometimes use cannabis gummies for sleep and recently increased to two or three evenings a week. You do not see this as a problem.

Identity, culture and belonging

If asked appropriately:

You are a cisgender Chinese Canadian woman and identify as bisexual. Having a girlfriend does not by itself tell someone your sexual identity.

If they follow up:

Your parents are welcoming toward Dani, but your mother sometimes calls her your “friend” around extended family. You find this painful but do not want every gathering to become a confrontation. You dislike assumptions that caregiving is simply “what Asian families do.” You value family while also noticing gendered expectations.

If asked about work experiences:

You have wondered whether descriptions such as “quiet” or “polite” were racialized and gendered, but you are not certain.

Supports, strengths and what matters

If asked:

Dani, close friends, your brother, stable housing, employment and health-care access are important resources. You communicate and organize well, have humour and interests, and advocate strongly for other people.

What is harder:

You are less comfortable asking others to accommodate you. Family, independence, dependability, Dani, doing good work and having an actual life all matter; these values are colliding.

What I want

If asked what should be different:

“I just want to figure out how to stop feeling like I'm failing at everything.”

If they follow up:

You want better sleep, less overwhelm, work boundaries, a sustainable way to help your parents, less conflict with Dani, time with friends and less guilt when doing something for yourself.

After the conversation

When the instructor calls time, use these prompts for paired or verbal reflection. Nothing is collected here.

  • What did the assessor's questions invite you to talk about?
  • What parts of your life stayed invisible?
  • Did you understand why you were being asked these questions?
  • Was there something you were waiting for them to ask?
