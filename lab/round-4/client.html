Round 4 · Client role

You are Alex Rivera

What you say first

“I'm just stretched really thin right now. I'm behind at school, work keeps changing my shifts, and everything costs too much. I don't want to drop out when I'm this close to finishing.”

How to play the client

Begin with only the opening statement.

When your partner asks about an area of your life, open the relevant section below.

Answer using the information provided. You do not need to tell them everything in the section at once. If they follow up, you can share more.

You may elaborate naturally when it is consistent with the scenario.

If information is not provided, you can say:

  • “I'm not sure.”
  • “I don't really know.”
  • “I'd rather not get into that right now.”
  • “Can you tell me why you're asking?”

Never use your own personal experiences to fill gaps in the scenario.

Your scenario information

Open a section when the assessor asks about that part of your life. Follow the cues inside each section.

Everyday life

If asked:

You are 24, creative, sarcastic and very online. You love graphic design, album artwork, typography and posters. You play volleyball and like horror films, thrift stores, tattoos and elaborate cooking when time and money allow. Five close friends are constantly in touch.

School and placement

If asked:

You are a talented final-year graphic design student with generally good grades and a portfolio you are proud of. Attendance has worsened and you may lose your placement.

If they follow up:

Work schedules, exhaustion, migraines, transportation, financial pressure, appointments and paid freelance jobs all contribute. Your supervisor sees a professionalism problem. You worry explaining everything will make you look unreliable.

Work

If asked:

You work 15–20 hours weekly at a café and do freelance design. Café shifts change with little notice. You often accept class-time shifts because you need money; freelance income varies.

Workplace experience:

A coworker repeatedly asked about your transition and surgery. You finally said, “My medical history isn't your workplace entertainment.” The questions mostly stopped.

Money, food and transportation

If asked generally:

You receive student aid, occasional family help and unstable earnings. High rent, tuition, supplies, transit, food and ordinary living costs continue even when hours are cut. You have about $2,700 in credit-card debt.

If they follow up:

You sometimes use the campus food bank but feel others need it more. Placement limits work hours; migraines disrupt shifts and deadlines. Your financial pressure exists independently of David.

Housing

If asked:

You rent a room with three others. Two are friends; one is barely known. The messy house is generally safe, repairs are slow and eviction is not imminent.

If asked about other options:

David once offered his condo. You declined because combining housing with the relationship would give him too much influence and make leaving harder.

Migraines and access needs

If asked:

You have migraine with aura, sometimes several during stress. Light and screens become unbearable, disrupting design work. Prescribed medication helps somewhat.

If they follow up about services:

A past clinician blamed testosterone even though migraines began years earlier. Your current physician treats migraines as migraines. You may want placement accommodations.

Family, friends and supports

If asked:

Your mother initially struggled with your transition but now consistently uses your name and pronouns and sends food. Your reserved father was supportive. You are close to your sister Sophie, 19, and have strong queer and trans friendships.

Culture:

You are Filipino and white Canadian. Extended-family gatherings and food matter. You choose who knows you are trans and dislike assumptions that your Filipino mother must be rejecting or “traditional.”

Gender, sexuality and belonging

If asked appropriately:

You are a trans man, use he/him pronouns and identify as queer. You mostly date men but do not identify as gay. Being trans matters but is not, in your view, the most interesting thing about you.

Privacy:

You share that you are trans when relevant; not everyone is entitled to know. Knowing you are trans does not reveal your anatomy, sexual practices or reproductive needs.

Experiences with institutions

If asked:

An old name remained in a placement system until you contacted three offices. A clinician fixated on hormones, and a gym employee questioned your change room. Your current physician, pharmacy, most professors and friends are affirming. Institutions are not uniformly hostile or uniformly supportive.

David and the arrangement

If asked about other money sources or an older man:

You have seen David, 52, two or three times monthly for eight months and sometimes call him your “sugar daddy.” You like and are attracted to him; you also value the money. He gives $300–$500 at times and has paid for groceries, phone bills and a school laptop.

If asked whether this is sex work:

“I don't know. Maybe technically? I don't really use that word for myself.” You do not call David a boyfriend and do not describe yourself as trafficked.

Material context:

Without the money you would probably see him less often. David helps with financial conditions he did not create.

Consent and boundaries

If asked generally:

David normally asks what you want. You have said no, changed your mind and once spent an evening watching television because of a migraine; he accepted it and still paid your cab.

If asked about pressure:

Once David suggested sex without a condom. You said no. He pushed once—“Come on, we've been seeing each other forever”—and you said no again. He stopped and used a condom. You were annoyed but do not call it assault.

Material constraint:

You once arranged a visit partly because you needed $400 rent. You say you would not make exactly the same choices with enough money, but do not describe yourself as coerced.

Control, exploitation and safety

If asked behaviourally:

David does not hold identification, control accounts or housing, monitor you, restrict movement, threaten you, take earnings, control testosterone, arrange other clients or require sex with others. You travel independently, can leave and believe he would be disappointed—not threatening—if you stopped.

Important distinction:

You answer respectful questions about actual conditions. If asked only, “Are you being trafficked?” you say, “No,” which reveals little about why.

Sexual health

If asked:

You obtain routine sexual-health screening, use condoms in some relationships and have discussed HIV prevention with a clinician. You are knowledgeable and do not need basic information repeated without a reason.

Strengths, what matters and what I want

Strengths:

You are an excellent designer, resourceful, funny, a good friend and able to advocate for yourself. Declining David's housing offer shows awareness of power and boundaries.

What matters:

Finishing school, creative and sexual autonomy, friends, Sophie, self-support, chosen relationships and not having every problem reduced to being trans.

What you want:

A workable financial plan, predictable work, placement help, migraine accommodations, completion of your portfolio, stable housing and the freedom to make your own decisions about David.

After the conversation

When the instructor calls time, use these prompts for paired or verbal reflection. Nothing is collected here.

  • What did the assessor's questions invite you to talk about?
  • What parts of your life stayed invisible?
  • Did you understand why you were being asked these questions?
  • Was there something you were waiting for them to ask?
