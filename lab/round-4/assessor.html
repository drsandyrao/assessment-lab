Round 4 · Assessor

Your role: assessor

Your purpose

Understand what is shaping the person's current situation, what they are trying to accomplish, what choices and constraints they are navigating, what resources are available, and how relationships, institutions and material conditions affect their options.

You do not need to ask every question or cover every area. Listen to the person and decide what is important to follow up.

Before you begin

  • How will you explain what you are trying to understand?
  • How will you begin?
  • What does the person need to understand about why you are asking?
  • Remember that this is a conversation, not a checklist.

Start at the centre: What is happening right now?

  • What is the person trying to accomplish?
  • What is getting in the way?
  • What do they identify as the problem?
  • What do they want?

Move outward across levels

Open each level to consider how it may shape the situation. These are areas for inquiry, not conclusions.

1. Personal resources & embodied life
  • health
  • disability or accessibility needs
  • knowledge
  • skills
  • coping
  • identities that matter to the person
  • aspirations
2. Relationships
  • family
  • friends
  • intimate or sexual relationships
  • financial relationships
  • support
  • dependency
  • power
3. Organizations & institutions
  • school or college
  • placement
  • workplace
  • health care
  • housing
  • financial aid
  • transportation
  • social services

What does this institution make easier?

What does it make harder?

4. Material conditions
  • income
  • rent
  • food
  • tuition
  • debt
  • employment security
  • transportation
  • access to health care
  • time
5. Identity & social location
  • gender
  • sexuality
  • race or culture
  • age
  • disability
  • class or economic position
  • other identities important to the person

Do not assume an identity matters in the same way in every setting. Ask when and how it becomes relevant.

6. Power
  • Who controls important resources?
  • Who makes decisions?
  • Who can impose consequences?
  • What happens if the person says no?
  • What choices are genuinely available?
  • How would greater access to money or resources change those choices?
  • Who benefits from current arrangements?
  • Where does the person have power?

These are areas for inquiry, not conclusions.

7. Structures
  • labour conditions
  • housing
  • education costs
  • health systems
  • gender norms
  • heteronormativity or cisnormativity
  • racism
  • ableism
  • economic inequality

Do not merely announce that a structure is operating. Look for information from the person or situation that supports its relevance.

8. Resources, resistance & possibilities
  • what the person is already doing
  • resources they already have
  • where they are exercising agency
  • relationships or institutions that are working well
  • possibilities for change at personal, relational, organizational or structural levels

Do not turn this assessment into a search for oppression. Attend to constraint, power, resources, privilege, agency, resistance and possibility.

If a transactional sexual relationship emerges:

  • do not assume that money or material support automatically establishes exploitation or trafficking
  • do not assume that describing an arrangement as consensual ends inquiry into consent, boundaries, material constraint, power or safety
  • assess actual conditions
  • use behaviourally specific questions where relevant

Before you decide you understand

  • What do I know because the person told me?
  • What am I inferring?
  • What have I not asked?
  • What might another assessment lens reveal?
After the conversation

When the instructor calls time, use these prompts for paired or verbal reflection. Nothing is collected here.

  • What did you learn that felt important?
  • What influenced what you asked next?
  • What do you still not know?
  • Was there something you hesitated or avoided asking?
