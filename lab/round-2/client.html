Round 2 · Client role

You are Marcus Thompson

What you say first

“I guess she got worried about something I said. I'm having a rough time, sure. But I don't know why everyone's making such a big deal out of it.”

How to play the client

Begin with only the opening statement.

When your partner asks about an area of your life, open the relevant section below.

Answer using the information provided. You do not need to tell them everything in the section at once. If they follow up, you can share more.

You may elaborate naturally when it is consistent with the scenario.

If information is not provided, you can say:

  • “I'm not sure.”
  • “I don't really know.”
  • “I'd rather not get into that right now.”
  • “Can you tell me why you're asking?”

Never use your own personal experiences to fill gaps in the scenario.

Your scenario information

Open a section when the assessor asks about that part of your life. Follow the cues inside each section.

Everyday life

If asked:

You are 46, a licensed electrician and father of Nia, 16, and Jordan, 12. You like basketball, grilling, old-school R&B, action movies, tools and arguing with Andre about sports. Your children groan at your dad jokes, which encourages you.

Injury, pain and health care

If asked generally:

Eight months ago you fell from a ladder at work and injured your lower back. Pain, sleep disruption and reduced mobility continue. You are on medical leave and still call yourself an electrician.

If they follow up:

Pain affects work, parenting, recreation and household tasks. You take prescribed pain medication, sometimes above the minimum but within the prescribed maximum, and worry about dependence. You attend physiotherapy consistently and have a good relationship with your nurse practitioner.

Work, money and housing

If asked:

Work gave you income, structure, connection and competence. You supervised apprentices and liked teaching them. Contact from coworkers has faded.

If they follow up:

Injury benefits are much lower than your wages. You have about three months of savings, carry a credit-card balance and feel the compensation system makes you prove you are hurt. Rent is current and housing is stable, but you have not told family how worried you are.

Separation and children

If asked:

Renee ended your 18-year relationship five months ago after a difficult period. There was no violence or threatening behaviour. You mostly communicate about the children.

If they follow up:

You were devastated and are embarrassed by how much it affected you. Nia is more distant. You cancelled two weekends with the children because of pain and feel guilty. Your children are deeply important, but that does not make questions about suicide unnecessary.

Emotional wellbeing

If asked generally:

You say you are frustrated, angry and tired more readily than sad. You have withdrawn from friends, lost interest in full basketball games, sleep poorly and sometimes stay in bed late.

If they follow the cue “I feel useless”:

Not working, seeing the children less and not knowing when life will change have damaged your sense of competence. You may say, “Everyone would be better off without me.”

Thoughts of suicide

If asked vaguely or leadingly:

If asked, “You're not going to hurt yourself, are you?” you say, “No, I'm fine.”

If asked directly and neutrally:

You pause, then say, “Yeah. Sometimes.” Thoughts began six weeks ago and now occur several times, especially late at night. Some are passive; others are explicitly about dying.

If they follow up about method, access and intent:

You have thought about taking a large amount of your prescribed medication and it is in your apartment. You have not chosen a date or time or worked out a detailed plan. You say, “I don't think I would actually do it,” but admit there have been nights when you wanted everything to stop.

The most concerning recent moment

Share only after direct follow-up about coming close, preparing, or the hardest recent moment:

Four nights ago, after a compensation letter and an argument with Renee, you had consumed three beers. At about 2 a.m. you went to the bathroom and looked at the medication bottle while thinking about taking all of it. You did not open it.

If asked what happened next:

You pictured Jordan learning you had died, put the medication down and texted Andre, “You awake?” Andre called. You talked about basketball for half an hour, but you did not tell him about suicide.

Planning, preparation and history

If asked:

You have not collected medication for suicide, written a note, given things away or made arrangements for dying. You have never attempted suicide.

Previous thoughts:

In your early twenties, after a breakup, you briefly thought about suicide but had no plan and did not seek treatment.

Today:

You have had less intense thoughts today but deny an immediate intention to kill yourself.

Self-harm

If asked directly:

You have never intentionally injured yourself to cope or regulate emotion. If the assessor uses “self-harm” as another word for suicidal thoughts, you become confused.

Alcohol and other substances

If asked generally:

You used to drink socially. Now you have two or three beers three or four evenings a week and drank more heavily twice recently.

If they follow up:

You had three beers during the bathroom episode. You do not use cannabis or non-prescribed drugs. You become defensive if your drinking is abruptly labelled a disorder.

Supports and help-seeking

If asked:

Andre lives 20 minutes away. You are close. Friends, a former coworker and your mother remain available, though you use less support lately.

If asked about getting help:

You attend physiotherapy, trust your nurse practitioner, previously participated seriously in couples counselling and once encouraged a grieving apprentice to use the employee assistance program. You do not see help-seeking as weakness.

Identity and experiences with services

If asked:

You are a Black Canadian cisgender heterosexual man. Some professionals have minimized your pain. You have wondered whether race played a part but do not claim to know.

Your concern in this encounter:

“What will happen if I tell you the truth about suicide?” You fear hospitalization, loss of control, or consequences for parenting that nobody has explained. You may ask, “Are you going to send me to the hospital now?”

Strengths, what matters and what I want

Strengths and values:

You are skilled, a mentor and an engaged father. You maintain relationships and reached toward connection by texting Andre during a dangerous moment. Your children, competence, independence, providing for family and being dependable matter deeply.

What you want:

At first: “I want my back fixed and my life back.” With more conversation, you want sleep, fewer frightening thoughts, presence with your children, clarity about work, and help without losing control over what happens next.

After the conversation

When the instructor calls time, use these prompts for paired or verbal reflection. Nothing is collected here.

  • What did the assessor's questions invite you to talk about?
  • What parts of your life stayed invisible?
  • Did you understand why you were being asked these questions?
  • Was there something you were waiting for them to ask?
