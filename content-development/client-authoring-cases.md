# Preliminary Client Authoring Cases

> **Development/authoring material — not for student-facing publication**

These whole-person authoring cases are the source material from which shorter student-facing client-role pages may eventually be developed. They are intentionally detailed and are not linked from the public website navigation.

The cases remain preliminary. The cross-case equity/stereotype audit is documented in `cross-case-audit.md`; an evidence review is still required before any case content is adapted for live Round 1–4 pages.

## Client A: Maya Chen
For now, I am treating this as our authoring version. Students will eventually see a much more carefully organized client-role version.
Age: 29
Pronouns: she/her
Gender: cisgender woman
Sexuality: bisexual
Racial/cultural identity: Chinese Canadian; born in Canada; parents immigrated to Canada before she was born
Location: Waterloo Region
Relationship: lives with her girlfriend, Dani
Work: communications coordinator for a mid-sized nonprofit
Reason for contact: Maya booked an appointment through a community health centre after seeing her family physician about headaches, poor sleep and feeling increasingly overwhelmed.
What Maya thinks is happening
Maya does not arrive thinking that she has a particular mental health condition.
Her basic explanation is:
“I'm just having a hard time keeping up with everything lately.”
Over approximately the past three months, she has become increasingly exhausted. She is sleeping poorly, getting headaches more often, having difficulty concentrating at work and sometimes feeling overwhelmed by things she would normally manage.
Her family physician suggested talking with the social worker at the community health centre. Maya agreed, although she is somewhat uncertain about what talking to a social worker is supposed to accomplish.
What she says first
This should be the only substantive information she volunteers automatically:
“I've just been really overwhelmed lately. I'm usually pretty good at keeping everything together, but the last few months have been a lot. I'm tired all the time, I'm not sleeping properly, and I feel like I'm falling behind on everything.”
Then she waits.
That is important. The assessor now has to decide where to go.
---
Her ordinary life
Maya is funny, organized and generally quite social. She likes cooking, live music, trying new restaurants, podcasts and going for long walks with Dani.
She has a small group of close friends from university. They have a group chat that is active almost every day.
She is devoted to an overcomplicated television baking competition and sends Dani running commentary about the judging even though she rarely bakes anything more ambitious than cookies.
She likes her job and is good at it. She particularly enjoys writing and campaign work.
She is usually the person in her family and friend group who remembers birthdays, makes reservations, organizes plans and figures things out when something goes wrong.
She does not describe herself as particularly “resilient.” She would probably say:
“I just get things done.”
---
Home and relationship
Maya has lived with Dani for about two years. They have been together for four years.
Their relationship is affectionate and generally supportive.
Dani has noticed that Maya is exhausted and has encouraged her to slow down. This sometimes causes arguments because Maya experiences it as one more person telling her what she should be doing.
There is no intimate partner violence.
If someone asks whether Maya feels safe at home, she can answer yes.
If someone assumes her partner is male, Maya notices. Depending on how it happens, she may simply correct them:
“My girlfriend.”
She doesn't necessarily become upset, but it affects how carefully she answers subsequent questions.
---
Family
Maya's parents live about 40 minutes away.
She has an older brother who lives in Alberta.
Her father has recently developed health problems. The diagnosis itself is manageable, but he has numerous appointments and has difficulty navigating some health information.
Maya has become the person who accompanies him to appointments, keeps track of information and helps her parents understand what is happening.
Her mother drives, but is uncomfortable driving on highways.
Her brother cares about their parents but cannot provide much practical assistance from Alberta.
Maya has never explicitly agreed to become the family care coordinator. It has gradually happened.
She loves her parents.
She is also exhausted by the responsibility.
Both things are true.
---
Work
Maya works full time.
There has recently been restructuring at her organization. One colleague left and has not been replaced.
Maya has absorbed some of that person's responsibilities.
She has started working through lunch and occasionally logs back in after dinner.
Her supervisor frequently tells her that she is “so dependable.”
Maya experiences this as both praise and a trap.
She has taken three sick days in the past six weeks, which is unusual for her.
She worries that colleagues are beginning to think she cannot manage her workload.
---
Money
Maya and Dani can pay their bills.
They are not experiencing food insecurity or imminent housing problems.
However, their rent increased recently and Maya has substantial student loan payments.
She also pays for some things for her parents without discussing it much with Dani.
Money is becoming a source of tension in their relationship.
Maya would describe herself as financially “fine.”
If someone asks more specifically about financial pressure, she acknowledges that she worries about money.
This gives us a useful distinction between:
“Do you have financial problems?”
and actually understanding someone's material circumstances.
---
Physical health
Maya has endometriosis.
This is an invisible chronic health condition.
She experiences pelvic pain, fatigue and occasionally severe menstrual pain. Some months are much worse than others.
She has previously felt dismissed by health professionals. Before receiving a diagnosis, she was repeatedly told that painful periods were normal.
She now has a physician she trusts reasonably well.
She takes medication as needed and has learned to work around flare-ups.
Recently, the combination of poor sleep, stress and pain has become harder to manage.
She does not automatically think of herself as disabled.
If asked whether she has a disability, she will probably say:
“No.”
If asked about chronic health conditions, pain or whether her health affects everyday activities, the assessor learns much more.
That is exactly the kind of assessment distinction we want.
---
Emotional wellbeing
Maya feels overwhelmed and irritable.
She sometimes cries unexpectedly when she gets home.
She has been worrying at night about work, her father, money and whether she is letting everyone down.
She has difficulty falling asleep because her mind keeps running through things she needs to do.
She does not currently have a mental health diagnosis.
She has never taken psychiatric medication.
She saw a counsellor for several sessions during university and found it somewhat helpful.
She does not describe herself as depressed.
If asked about enjoyment, she says she still enjoys things when she actually has time to do them.
---
Suicide
Maya is not currently suicidal.
If asked directly:
“No, I haven't been thinking about killing myself.”
If asked about wishing she could disappear, not wake up, or escape everything, there is more nuance.
About two weeks ago, after an especially difficult day, she lay in bed and thought:
“I wish I could just stop existing for a while.”
She did not want to die. She did not consider killing herself. There was no plan or intent.
She was expressing exhaustion and wanting everything to stop.
This gives us useful material for teaching direct follow-up without manufacturing a suicide crisis.
---
Self-harm
Maya does not currently self-harm and has no history of intentional self-injury.
If asked directly, she says no.
Again, every sensitive question does not need to uncover a hidden positive response. Students need experience asking important questions even when the answer is no.
---
Alcohol and other substances
Maya drinks socially, usually one or two drinks when out with friends.
She occasionally uses cannabis gummies to help herself sleep.
She has been using them somewhat more frequently recently, perhaps two or three evenings per week.
She does not view this as a problem.
Whether it is clinically important remains something the assessor has to consider rather than something the case tells them.

Gender, sexuality and belonging
Maya identifies as bisexual.
Her parents know Dani as her partner and are polite and welcoming toward her.
Maya has never explicitly discussed the word bisexual with them.
Her mother occasionally refers to Dani as Maya's “friend” when speaking to extended family.
Maya finds this irritating and painful, but she generally lets it go.
Dani thinks Maya should correct her mother.
Maya does not want every family interaction to become a confrontation.
There is no clean story here of either acceptance or rejection.
 
Race, culture and institutional experiences
Maya identifies strongly as Chinese Canadian.
She dislikes assumptions that she is responsible for her parents because “that's just Asian families.”
Family responsibility is important to her, but she also experiences gendered expectations within her family and feels that some responsibilities have fallen to her because she is the daughter.
At work, she has occasionally been described as “quiet” or “polite” in ways she thinks may be racialized and gendered.
She isn't certain.
This is something she has thought about rather than something she presents as an established fact.
That ambiguity is useful.
 
Strengths and resources
Maya has considerable resources.
She has:
- a supportive partner;
- close friends;
- stable housing;
- employment she generally values;
- health-care access;
- strong communication skills;
- considerable organizational ability;
- insight into her circumstances;
- a history of seeking help when she decides she needs it;
- a good relationship with her brother despite the distance;
- humour;
- interests and activities she enjoys.
She is also very good at advocating for other people.
She is considerably less comfortable asking other people to accommodate her.
 
What matters to Maya
Maya values family.
She values independence.
She values being dependable.
She values her relationship with Dani.
She wants to be good at her job.
She wants enough time and energy to have an actual life outside her responsibilities.
These values are beginning to collide with one another.
 
What Maya wants
If someone asks:
“What would you like to be different?”
she does not say “I want treatment for anxiety.”
She says something closer to:
“I just want to figure out how to stop feeling like I'm failing at everything.”
If explored further, she wants:
- better sleep;
- less overwhelm;
- some boundaries around work;
- a more sustainable way of helping her parents;
- less conflict with Dani;
- time to see her friends again;
- to stop feeling guilty whenever she does something for herself.
 
What people could easily get wrong about Maya
This is important for our teaching design.
Assumption: Her family is burdensome.
Reality: Maya deeply values caring for her parents. She wants support making the responsibility sustainable, not necessarily less connection to them.
Assumption: Endometriosis is simply a medical-history item.
Reality: pain and fatigue materially affect her current functioning.
Assumption: Being employed full time means finances are irrelevant.
Reality: there is meaningful financial pressure.
Assumption: Having a girlfriend tells us Maya's sexual identity.
Reality: it doesn't. She identifies as bisexual.
Assumption: Her statement about wanting to “stop existing” means she is suicidal.
Reality: direct assessment establishes something more nuanced.
Assumption: Being highly capable means she has adequate capacity for all of her current responsibilities.
Reality: capability and available capacity are different things.
Maya's disclosure map
This is where the case starts becoming usable in Assessment Lab.
She says first: overwhelmed, exhausted, poor sleep, falling behind.
Readily available if asked: job, girlfriend, parents, father's health, friendships, basic physical health, sleep, previous counselling, ordinary interests.
Requires follow-up: increased workload; caregiving burden; financial pressure; cannabis use for sleep; worsening endometriosis symptoms; conflict with Dani; gendered family expectations.
Sensitive but available through respectful inquiry: bisexual identity; passive “stop existing” thought; experiences she perceives as possibly racialized.
May decline: questions she experiences as unnecessarily personal or unrelated to the purpose of the assessment.
Not present: current suicidal intent, suicide plan, suicide attempt history, current self-harm, intimate partner violence, food insecurity, unstable housing.
That last line matters enormously. Not every domain contains a revelation.
I think Maya passes our basic quality test: she has an ordinary life, substantial strengths, privilege and constraint, intersecting identities, an invisible physical health condition, relationships that cannot be categorized as simply supportive/problematic, potentially sensitive information, and several places where listening and follow-up matter.
Most importantly, there is no single secret explanation for Maya.

## Client B: Marcus Thompson
Age: 46
Pronouns: he/him
Gender: cisgender man
Sexuality: heterosexual
Racial identity: Black Canadian
Location: Kitchener-Waterloo
Relationship: separated; two children
Work: licensed electrician, currently on medical leave
Reason for contact: Marcus is meeting a social worker in a primary-care/community health setting after telling his nurse practitioner, “Sometimes I don't see the point anymore.”
The nurse practitioner asked whether Marcus would speak with the social worker before leaving.
This is important: Marcus has not arrived asking for a suicide assessment. He came for an ordinary health appointment.
What Marcus thinks is happening
Marcus came to the clinic for follow-up after a workplace injury.
Eight months ago, he fell from a ladder at work and injured his back. He expected to recover and return to work relatively quickly. Instead, he continues to experience significant pain.
His life has become much smaller.
He isn't sleeping properly. He's frustrated with the compensation process. Money is getting tighter. His relationship ended during the same general period. He isn't working. He sees his children less than he used to.
During his appointment, his nurse practitioner asked how he was coping.
Marcus said:
“I'm tired of all of it. Some days I honestly don't see the point anymore.”
She asked him to speak with the social worker.
Marcus agreed, although part of him now wishes he had kept his mouth shut.
What he says first
When the student assessor begins, Marcus says:
“I guess she got worried about something I said. I'm having a rough time, sure. But I don't know why everyone's making such a big deal out of it.”
Then he waits.
This forces the assessor to explain why they are there and eventually ask directly about suicide.
 
His ordinary life
Before his injury, Marcus's life was busy.
He has worked as an electrician for more than 20 years and takes considerable pride in his trade.
He likes fixing things. Friends and family frequently call him when something breaks.
He follows basketball closely. He likes grilling, old-school R&B, action movies and arguing with his brother about sports.
His children groan at his dad jokes, which only encourages him. He owns more tools than his apartment storage can reasonably accommodate and is particular about lending them out.
He has two children:
Nia, 16, and Jordan, 12.
They are extremely important to him.
Before his injury and separation, much of his week revolved around work and the children.
 
Home
Marcus currently lives alone in a two-bedroom apartment.
The second bedroom is for his children.
Since the separation, they usually stay with him every second weekend, although this has become less consistent.
His apartment is reasonably stable. He is not facing immediate eviction.
However, his income has decreased substantially while he is off work.
He has started using savings to cover expenses.
 
Relationship and separation
Marcus and his former partner, Renee, were together for 18 years.
They separated approximately five months ago.
The relationship had been strained before Marcus's injury. His pain, irritability, financial stress and being at home constantly intensified existing problems.
The separation was Renee's decision.
Marcus was devastated.
They currently communicate mostly about the children.
There is no intimate partner violence.
There were arguments, sometimes loud ones, but neither partner physically harmed or threatened the other.
Marcus is embarrassed that the relationship ended.
He has told relatively few people how badly the separation affected him.
 
His children
Marcus loves being a father.
Nia has become somewhat distant recently. Marcus isn't sure whether this is ordinary adolescence, anger about the separation, or discomfort around his mood.
Jordan still wants to spend considerable time with him.
Marcus has cancelled two planned weekends because his pain was particularly bad.
He feels guilty about this.
If asked what keeps him going when things are especially difficult, his children are the first thing he identifies.
But we should be careful here: children are meaningful to him, but they are not a magical protective factor that eliminates suicide risk.
 
Work and identity
Work is enormously important to Marcus.
It provides income, structure, social connection and a sense of competence.
He supervises apprentices and enjoys teaching younger workers.
Since his injury, he has had very little contact with coworkers.
At first they checked in frequently. That has gradually decreased.
Marcus sometimes wonders whether everyone has simply moved on without him.
He feels embarrassed when people ask when he is returning because he doesn't know.
If asked what he does for work, he still says:
“I'm an electrician.”
He does not say that he is unemployed.
 
Physical health and pain
Marcus has persistent lower-back pain following the workplace injury.
Some days are manageable. Other days are significantly worse.
Sitting or standing for extended periods is difficult.
Sleep is disrupted by pain.
He has gained some weight since the injury and is much less physically active.
He takes prescribed pain medication.
He sometimes takes more than the minimum prescribed dose on particularly difficult days, but he reports staying within the prescribed maximum.
He worries about becoming dependent on medication.
He has not discussed that worry with his prescriber.
Marcus has attended physiotherapy consistently, although progress has been slower than he hoped. He has a good working relationship with his nurse practitioner and generally trusts her, which is part of why he answered honestly when she asked how he was coping.
 
Disability
Marcus does not identify as disabled.
If asked:
“Do you have a disability?”
he says:
“No. I'm injured.”
He assumes he will eventually recover enough to return to work.
However, the injury currently affects employment, sleep, mobility, parenting, recreation, household tasks and social participation.
This gives us another useful example of the difference between identity, impairment and functioning.
 
Money
Marcus is receiving workplace injury benefits, but his income is substantially lower than when he was working.
The compensation process has been frustrating.
He recently received a request for additional documentation and interpreted it as another suggestion that he needs to prove he is actually injured.
He has approximately three months of savings remaining at his current spending rate.
He has begun carrying a balance on his credit card.
He has not missed rent.
He has not told his family how worried he is about money.
 
Emotional wellbeing
Marcus describes himself as frustrated, angry and tired more readily than sad.
If asked directly about depression, he says:
“I don't know. Maybe. I'm just sick of everything.”
He has lost interest in some things he normally enjoys.
He rarely watches full basketball games anymore.
He has stopped answering some friends' calls.
His appetite varies.
He has difficulty sleeping.
He sometimes stays in bed considerably later than he intends.
He feels increasingly useless.
The word useless matters. If the assessor follows it, more becomes available.
 
Suicide
This is the central sensitive-assessment component.
Marcus has been experiencing suicidal thoughts.
He does not volunteer the phrase “suicidal thoughts.”
If the assessor asks only:
“You're not going to hurt yourself, are you?”
Marcus says:
“No, I'm fine.”
If asked directly and neutrally whether he has been thinking about suicide or killing himself, he pauses and then says:
“Yeah. Sometimes.”
That is not the end of the assessment.
Frequency
The thoughts began approximately six weeks ago.
Initially they were occasional.
During the past two weeks, they have occurred several times.
They are generally strongest late at night when he cannot sleep.
Nature of the thoughts
Sometimes the thoughts are passive:
“Everyone would probably be better off without me.”
At other times they are more explicitly about dying.
Planning
Marcus has thought about taking a large amount of his medication.
He has not worked out a detailed plan.
He has not selected a date or time.
Access
He has prescribed medication in his apartment.
The assessor therefore needs to understand access rather than assuming that absence of a detailed plan means absence of concern.
Intent
Marcus says:
“I don't think I would actually do it.”
If asked how certain he is:
“Most of the time I'm pretty sure. But there have been nights where I just wanted everything to stop.”
This is deliberately not a clean yes/no answer.
Preparatory behaviour
He has not collected medication for the purpose of suicide.
He has not written a note.
He has not given possessions away.
He has not made arrangements in anticipation of dying.
Previous suicide attempts
None.
Previous suicidal thoughts
He remembers briefly thinking about suicide in his early twenties after a relationship ended, but never made a plan and never sought treatment.
Most concerning recent moment
Four nights ago, after receiving another compensation-related letter and arguing with Renee on the phone, Marcus was awake at approximately 2:00 a.m.
He thought about taking all of his medication.
He went into the bathroom and looked at the bottle.
He did not open it.
After several minutes he returned to bed.
This is the most important information in the case.
He will not volunteer it immediately.
It requires direct questions about whether he has come close to acting on the thoughts, what has happened during the most difficult moments, or whether he has ever found himself preparing to do something.
 
What happened that night
Marcus thought about his children.
More specifically, he imagined Jordan finding out that his father had died.
He put the medication down.
He texted his brother:
“You awake?”
His brother called him.
Marcus did not tell his brother he had been thinking about suicide. He said he couldn't sleep.
They talked about basketball for approximately half an hour.
Marcus eventually went to sleep.
That interaction matters because it shows both connection and concealment.
 
Current state
Today, Marcus denies an immediate intention to kill himself.
He has had suicidal thoughts today, but they are less intense than four nights ago.
He is somewhat relieved to be talking about it once the conversation gets going.
He is also worried about what will happen because he disclosed it.
He may ask:
“Are you going to send me to the hospital now?”
This is an excellent moment for students to practise explaining what they are doing instead of making promises or threats.
 
Self-harm
Marcus has never intentionally injured himself as a way of coping or regulating emotion.
If asked whether he self-harms:
“No.”
If the assessor uses “self-harm” to mean his suicidal thoughts, Marcus becomes confused.
This gives us an opportunity to teach the distinction.
 
Alcohol
Before his injury, Marcus generally drank socially.
Recently he has started drinking alone more often.
Approximately three or four evenings per week he has two or three beers.
On two recent occasions he drank considerably more.
Four nights ago, when he looked at his medication, he had consumed three beers.
This matters to the suicide assessment.
Marcus does not think he has an alcohol problem.
He may become somewhat defensive if the assessor abruptly labels his drinking problematic.
 
Other substances
He does not use cannabis or other non-prescribed drugs.
He takes his prescribed medication.
 
Social support
Marcus's younger brother, Andre, lives approximately 20 minutes away.
They are close.
Andre knows Marcus is struggling but does not know about the suicidal thoughts.
Marcus also has several friends, although he has increasingly withdrawn from them.
One former coworker texts him every week.
Marcus usually replies but rarely initiates contact.
His mother lives in Toronto. They speak approximately once a week.
He has more social support available than he is currently using.
 
Race, autonomy and help-seeking
Marcus is Black.
He has had experiences with health-care professionals where he felt his pain was minimized.
He is unsure whether race played a role and does not make a definitive claim about it.
Marcus has appropriately used health and support services at other points in his life. In addition to his current physiotherapy and primary care, he and Renee participated in couples counselling several years ago. He did not find every session useful, but he engaged seriously and does not regard seeking support as weakness.
He once encouraged an apprentice who was struggling after a family death to use the employee assistance program and checked in afterward.
His hesitation in this encounter is not a general inability to discuss emotion and should not be explained by his race or gender. His central concern is: “What will happen if I tell you the truth about suicide?”
He does not know whether disclosure will mean being sent to hospital, losing control over decisions, being viewed as an unsafe parent, or facing consequences nobody has explained. Transparency about why questions are being asked and what happens with the information is therefore essential to the encounter.
 
Strengths and capabilities
Marcus is highly skilled in his trade.
He is an engaged father.
He is a mentor.
He has maintained important relationships.
He has previously managed difficult periods in his life.
He sought medical care for his injury.
He has attended physiotherapy, maintained a constructive relationship with his nurse practitioner, participated in couples counselling and encouraged another person to seek mental-health support.
He agreed to speak with the social worker even though he was uncomfortable.
Four nights ago, during a highly concerning moment, he interrupted the sequence himself and reached toward connection by texting Andre.
That does not erase risk. It tells us something important about his capacities and available pathways toward safety.
 
What matters to Marcus
His children.
Being dependable.
Work and competence.
Independence.
Providing for his family.
His relationship with his brother.
Being someone other people can count on.
His current circumstances threaten several of these simultaneously.
 
What Marcus wants
Initially:
“I want my back fixed and I want my life back.”
If the conversation develops:
He wants to sleep.
He wants the suicidal thoughts to stop frightening him.
He wants to be more present with his children.
He wants clarity about whether he will return to his previous work.
He wants help without losing control over what happens next.
That last part is particularly important.
 
What people could get wrong about Marcus
“He says he wouldn't do it, so the risk is low.”
That conclusion would ignore the recent episode involving medication.
“He has children, so he won't die by suicide.”
His children matter enormously and influenced a recent decision. That does not make further assessment unnecessary.
“He has a suicide plan.”
That may overstate what we know. He has considered a method and has access to it; the assessor needs precision about planning, intent and behaviour.
“He drinks because he has an alcohol problem.”
We know his alcohol use has increased and was present during a concerning episode. We need more information before assigning a disorder.
“His injury caused everything.”
The injury, relationship separation, financial uncertainty, disrupted identity, isolation, pain and institutional processes interact.
“He doesn't want help.”
Marcus is ambivalent about help and worried about losing autonomy. He still agreed to the conversation.
“Marcus is reluctant because Black men do not seek help or discuss emotion.”
That would be a stereotype contradicted by his history. His current hesitation is specifically about uncertainty, autonomy and the possible consequences of disclosing suicidal thoughts.
 
Marcus's disclosure map
SAYS FIRST
The nurse practitioner got worried. Things have been rough. He doesn't understand why everyone is making such a big deal about what he said.
SHARES IF ASKED
Injury, pain, medical leave, separation, children, sleep difficulties, work, brother, basic alcohol use.
CUE → FOLLOW UP
“I'm useless.”
“I don't see the point.”
“Everyone would be better off.”
“My kids deserve better.”
“I'm sick of proving I'm hurt.”
“I've been drinking a little more.”
Each opens a different pathway.
SENSITIVE BUT AVAILABLE WHEN ASKED DIRECTLY
Suicidal thoughts.
Thinking about medication.
Access to medication.
The bathroom episode four nights ago.
Increased alcohol use.
Previous suicidal thoughts.
His fear that disclosure will result in hospitalization.
His uncertainty about effects on autonomy, decision-making and parenting.
Experiences of having pain minimized.
NOT PRESENT
Previous suicide attempt.
Non-suicidal self-injury.
Current intimate partner violence.
Psychosis.
Housing instability.
Complete social isolation.
A predetermined intention to die.
## Client C: Evelyn Bennett
Age: 68
Pronouns: she/her
Gender: cisgender woman
Sexuality: lesbian
Racial/cultural identity: white Canadian
Relationship: married to Joanne, 71
Work: retired elementary-school teacher
Location: Waterloo Region
Reason for contact: Evelyn is meeting a social worker through an outpatient hospital program following a recent fall and wrist fracture.
The referral notes that Evelyn seems worried about returning home and that there was some tension between Evelyn and her spouse during discharge planning.
The social worker has been asked to understand Evelyn's home situation, supports and whether there are any concerns affecting a safe return home.
What Evelyn thinks is happening
Evelyn thinks the social worker is there because “hospitals like to make sure old people can manage at home.”
She dislikes being treated as frail.
Her wrist is healing. She is temporarily having difficulty with cooking, bathing, driving and some household activities, but she expects this to improve.
She wants to go home.
She also feels apprehensive about it.
She does not initially explain why.
What she says first
“I'm managing. My wrist is a nuisance, but it'll heal. Joanne's at home, so I'm not going back to an empty house or anything. I really don't think I need a whole plan made for me.”
Then she waits.
---
Evelyn's ordinary life
Evelyn taught elementary school for 34 years.
She loved teaching and still meets three former colleagues for lunch every month.
She reads constantly, gardens, watches terrible murder mysteries, does the crossword every morning and is an enthusiastic baker.
She volunteers twice a month with a community literacy program.
She and Joanne have a small dog named Archie.
Evelyn has an adult daughter, Simone, from a previous marriage, and two grandchildren.
Her grandchildren call Joanne “Jo.”
Evelyn has a full life that she very much wants to continue living.
---
Home
Evelyn and Joanne own a townhouse.
They have lived there for 14 years.
The mortgage is paid off.
There are stairs to the bedrooms and main bathroom. Evelyn is currently finding them difficult because of her wrist and lingering soreness from the fall.
She has no immediate housing insecurity.
She wants to remain in her home.
---
Her relationship with Joanne
Evelyn and Joanne have been together for 22 years and married for 11.
There is real affection in the relationship.
They have travelled together, supported one another through illness, helped raise Evelyn's grandchildren and built a life together.
Joanne was previously outgoing, funny and highly social.
Over approximately the past two years, Joanne has changed.
She has become more irritable and suspicious.
She sometimes accuses Evelyn of hiding things from her.
She has begun checking where Evelyn is going and asking when she will be home.
She dislikes some of Evelyn's friends and complains when Evelyn spends time with them.
Evelyn has gradually reduced some of her social activities because it is easier than dealing with an argument afterward.
This is not how the relationship was for most of its history.
---
What has been happening recently
Over the past six months, Joanne's behaviour has become more concerning.
She sometimes takes Evelyn's phone and looks through her messages.
She has accused Evelyn of having an affair with a former colleague. Evelyn is not having an affair.
During arguments, Joanne has stood in doorways and prevented Evelyn from leaving a room until the conversation was “finished.”
Joanne has called Evelyn selfish and a liar.
On one occasion, approximately two months ago, Joanne grabbed Evelyn's forearm hard enough to leave bruising.
Joanne apologized afterward and cried.
There have been no other incidents of grabbing or hitting that Evelyn identifies.
Evelyn does not use the term abuse to describe her marriage.
If asked:
“Is Joanne abusive?”
Evelyn is likely to say:
“No. She's not an abusive person.”
That answer does not end the assessment.
---
The fall
Evelyn fractured her wrist after falling on the stairs at home.
Joanne did not push her.
This is deliberate.
We should not make every suspicious circumstance turn out to be interpersonal violence.
Evelyn was carrying laundry, missed a step and fell.
Joanne called the ambulance.
However, when clinicians repeatedly asked Evelyn whether she had been pushed, she became irritated.
She felt they had decided what happened before listening to her.
This gives us an important lesson:
Concern about interpersonal violence does not justify turning suspicion into fact.
---
Why Evelyn is apprehensive about returning home
She expects Joanne will become even more controlling while Evelyn needs assistance.
Evelyn hates feeling physically dependent on her.
She is also worried about Joanne's behaviour more generally.
But Evelyn is worried about Joanne, too.
Something seems wrong.
---
Joanne's health
Joanne has not been formally diagnosed with a cognitive disorder.
Over the past year, Evelyn has noticed that Joanne:
- repeats questions;
- misplaces things and accuses others of moving them;
- occasionally becomes confused while driving somewhere familiar;
- has difficulty managing online banking that she previously handled;
- has become more suspicious;
