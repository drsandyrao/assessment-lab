# Cross-Case Equity and Stereotype Audit

> **Development/authoring material — not for student-facing publication**

The four preliminary cases were reviewed together as a set, rather than only as individual cases. This matters because patterns that seem defensible in one case can create unintended associations when repeated across the full activity.

## Distribution of racial identities

### Concern

The original four clients were all racialized. As a set, this risked implicitly associating racialized people with being the subjects of social-work assessment. It also risked framing structural and intersectional thinking as relevant only to racialized people.

### Response and pedagogical reason

Evelyn was revised from Black Caribbean Canadian to white Canadian and renamed Evelyn Bennett for consistency with the revised background. Her case continues to include structural dimensions involving age and ageism, gender, sexuality, historical experiences of being lesbian within institutions, health-care power, interpersonal power, autonomy and financial resources.

This distribution allows students to encounter structural dimensions in the life of a white client and makes clear that structural/intersectional assessment is not limited to racialized people.

## LGBTQ+ identities and adversity

### Concern

The original set placed sexual or relationship trauma, or highly sensitive sexual material, alongside all three LGBTQ+ clients.

### Response and pedagogical reason

Maya's sexual-assault history and all connected disclosure material were removed without replacing them with another trauma. Her bisexual identity remains an ordinary part of who she is, including the teaching point that having a girlfriend does not establish her sexual identity.

This change interrupts an unnecessary association between bisexuality and sexual trauma while preserving Maya's complexity, ordinary life, relationships, chronic health condition, material circumstances and nuanced passive thought about wanting everything to stop.

## Marcus and Black masculinity

### Concern

The previous wording could imply that Black masculinity explains reluctance to seek help or discuss emotion.

### Response and pedagogical reason

Marcus now has an explicit history of appropriate help-seeking: engaging in physiotherapy and primary care, participating in couples counselling, and encouraging an apprentice to seek mental-health support. His experiences of having pain minimized remain nuanced; he does not claim race as the definitive explanation when he does not know.

His present hesitation is specifically about autonomy, uncertainty about professional responses to suicide disclosure, possible hospitalization, parenting consequences and loss of control. This creates an opportunity for the assessor to practise transparency about why questions are being asked and what will happen with the information.

## Alex, trans identity and transactional sex

### Concern

Alex's trans and queer identities and his relationship with David could be allowed to define the case or make David appear to be the cause of Alex's financial precarity. The case must also avoid treating Alex as a hidden trafficking victim.

### Response and pedagogical reason

Alex remains trans and queer, and the sugar-daddy/transactional sexual relationship remains because it supports important assessment learning about sexuality, consent, sex work, power, material conditions, exploitation and trafficking. The case retains clear evidence of Alex exercising consent and boundaries, the condom-pressure incident, David's housing offer, and the current absence of trafficking indicators.

The financial material now makes explicit that Alex's precarity arises independently through high rent, education costs, student financial assistance, low and unpredictable café wages, variable freelance income, migraines, placement demands, transportation, food and ordinary living costs. David's money helps Alex navigate these conditions; David did not create them.

The central structural question is: **What conditions shape the choices available to Alex?** It is not: **Why does Alex have a sugar daddy?** Calling the arrangement consensual also does not end assessment of consent, boundaries, material constraint, sexual health, power or safety.

## Ordinary life

### Concern

Assessment cases can reduce people to presenting concerns, diagnoses, trauma, risk or marginalization.

### Response and pedagogical reason

Every case retains ordinary, non-problem-focused information, interests, humour, relationships, preferences and activities. Maya has cooking, restaurants, music, friends and a favourite baking competition. Marcus has basketball, grilling, music, tools, family humour and dad jokes. Evelyn has teaching, gardening, murder mysteries, baking, crosswords, friendships, volunteering and family. Alex has design, volleyball, horror films, thrift stores, friends, cooking, tattoos and his portfolio.

Students should encounter people whose lives exceed the purpose of assessment.

## Status and boundaries

These remain preliminary authoring cases. The live Round 1–4 pages have not been updated. No assessor guidance, new diagnosis or replacement trauma was added, and the four proposed assessment assignments remain unchanged.
