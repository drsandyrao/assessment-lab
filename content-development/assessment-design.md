# Assessment Design

> **Development/authoring material — not for student-facing publication**

## Central teaching proposition

**What we ask shapes what we come to know.**

## Current proposed four-round structure

### Round 1 — Maya Chen

**Proposed lens:** Comprehensive psychosocial/biopsychosocial assessment

**Core practice challenge:** Breadth, curiosity, purposeful inquiry, deciding what to follow up.

### Round 2 — Marcus Thompson

**Proposed lens:** Suicide assessment, including differentiation and assessment of self-harm

**Core practice challenge:** Asking directly about suicide, following up precisely, assessing changes over time, intent, planning/preparation, access to means, previous behaviour, supports, current circumstances, and tolerating difficult answers without prematurely determining a level of risk.

### Round 3 — Evelyn Bennett

**Proposed lens:** Interpersonal safety / trauma-sensitive assessment

**Core practice challenge:** Asking behaviourally specific questions about safety, violence, coercion and consent; distinguishing current and historical experiences; responding to disclosure; respecting boundaries; preserving autonomy; and avoiding premature conclusions.

### Round 4 — Alex Rivera

**Proposed lens:** Structural/intersectional assessment

**Core practice challenge:** Understanding identities, material conditions, institutions, power, discrimination, resources, agency and structural constraint without reducing the person to their identities or assuming exploitation.

## Emerging cross-round principles

- The person exists beyond the assessment.
- The assessment lens belongs to the assessor, not to the person.
- Every client is a whole person with ordinary life, relationships, identities, health, resources, strengths, material circumstances, institutional experiences, hopes and uncertainties.
- No client should be defined by diagnosis, trauma, suicide, disability, marginalization or another single characteristic.
- Not every sensitive question should uncover a hidden positive answer.
- Assessment involves distinguishing what is known, what is inferred, and what remains unknown.
- Students should practise asking sensitive questions directly and respectfully.
- Behaviourally specific questions may produce more useful information than labels.
- Disclosure does not create entitlement to unnecessary detail.
- The person may decline to answer.
- Assessment should attend to strengths and resources as well as concerns.
- Different assessment purposes make different information visible.
- No single assessment encounter provides complete knowledge of a person.
- The final activity should not imply that one assessment approach is the universally “best” approach.

## Disclosure architecture

1. **SAY FIRST** — information volunteered at the beginning.
2. **SHARE IF ASKED** — information readily provided when the relevant area is opened.
3. **CUE → FOLLOW UP** — an initial answer contains a cue; more becomes available if the assessor notices and follows up.
4. **SENSITIVE / CONTEXT MATTERS** — information available through direct, respectful, purposeful inquiry.
5. **MAY DECLINE** — information the client does not want to discuss or does not need to disclose.
